Andhra Pradesh: Rainfall and Crop Production (EDA)¶

I study in Guntur, and in this region almost every conversation about farming comes back to two things: how much it rained and what the districts grow. I wanted to check those with real data.

Data

  • IMD sub-division monthly rainfall, 1901-2017 (data.gov.in). AP is split into two sub-divisions: Coastal Andhra Pradesh and Rayalaseema.
  • District-wise, season-wise crop production for AP, 1997-2014 (data.gov.in). Area is in hectares and production in tonnes.

Questions

  1. How much rain does each region get, and when?
  2. Is rainfall going up or down over 117 years? How often are there drought years?
  3. Which crops and districts matter most?
  4. Does rainfall actually affect crop yield?

Observations

  • In Coastal AP the wettest month is actually October (184 mm), not July or August. That is the north-east monsoon and the cyclones from the Bay of Bengal.
  • Rayalaseema depends on the north-east monsoon even more: about 35% of its rain comes in Oct-Dec, compared to 26% for Coastal AP.
  • So for AP, a "normal monsoon" in June-September is only part of the story.

Long-term trend¶

Coastal Andhra Pradesh: 1901-1930 avg 1019 mm, 1988-2017 avg 1079 mm, variation (CV) 18.1%
Rayalaseema: 1901-1930 avg 765 mm, 1988-2017 avg 810 mm, variation (CV) 19.7%

The trend line goes up slightly (about +4 mm per decade) but the p-value is far above 0.05, so I can't say rainfall is really increasing. The year-to-year swing is much bigger than any trend: the coefficient of variation is around 18-20%.

Drought and excess years¶

IMD calls a year "deficient" when rain is more than 20% below normal and "excess" when it is more than 20% above. I used the 1901-2017 average as normal.

Rayalaseema had 18 deficient years out of 117 (about 1 in 6.5) while Coastal AP had 12. Rayalaseema also has lower rain to begin with, so a bad year hits it harder. 2002 was the driest year on record for Coastal AP (703 mm).

  • The two Godavari districts, Krishna and Guntur grow about 63% of AP's rice. These are the delta districts with canal irrigation.
  • Guntur alone produces 57.7% of AP's dry chillies. No surprise for anyone who has seen the Guntur chilli yard.

3. Does rainfall affect yield?¶

Yield = production / area (tonnes per hectare). I compared yearly yield with that year's rainfall for two cases:

  • Groundnut (Kharif) in the 4 Rayalaseema districts vs Rayalaseema June-September rain. Mostly rain-fed.
  • Rice in the 9 coastal districts vs Coastal AP annual rain. Mostly irrigated.

Only 18 years overlap (1997-2014), so these are rough numbers.

Groundnut (Rayalaseema) yield vs Jun-Sep rain: r = 0.77, p = 0.0002

This was the most interesting result:

  • Groundnut yield in Rayalaseema moves with the monsoon (r = 0.77, p < 0.001). In 2007 (665 mm) the yield was 1.37 t/ha. In 2006 and 2008 (about 340 mm each) it fell to about 0.23 t/ha. That's a 6x swing for the same crop on almost the same land, which explains a lot about farm distress in Anantapur.
  • Coastal rice yield shows no real link with rainfall (r = -0.27, not significant). The delta gets water from the Godavari and Krishna canals, so local rain matters much less.

4. Summary¶

Rice share of Godavari, Krishna, Guntur districts 63.4%
Guntur share of AP dry chillies 57.7%
Groundnut yield vs monsoon rain (Rayalaseema) r = 0.77
Rice yield vs rain (coastal) r = -0.27 (no link)

What it means: irrigation is what separates the two regions. Rain-fed Rayalaseema farmers carry the full risk of a bad monsoon, so things like crop insurance, drip irrigation and drought-tolerant crops matter most there.

Limitations: crop data only goes to 2014 and has 18 years, the rainfall is at sub-division level (not district), and correlation does not prove the rain alone caused the change in yield.
