Andhra Pradesh · 1901–2017

When the monsoon decides the harvest

117 years of rainfall, 18 years of district crop data, and one question: does the rain really make or break a farmer's year in AP? It depends a lot on which side of the state you live in.

By Tejdeep Kellampalli · data from IMD and data.gov.in · analysis in Python

Andhra Pradesh is really two climates sharing one name. Coastal Andhra runs along the Bay of Bengal from Srikakulam to Nellore, with the Godavari and Krishna deltas in the middle. Rayalaseema (Kurnool, Anantapur, Kadapa and Chittoor) sits inland, in the rain shadow of the Western Ghats.

I used the India Meteorological Department's sub-division rainfall series (1901–2017) and the district-wise crop production statistics (1997–2014) to look at how different they are, and whether that difference shows up in the fields.

On average, coastal AP gets 1050 mm of rain a year. Rayalaseema gets only 765 mm, about a quarter less.

Most of India waits for the south-west monsoon from June to September. Here it brings less than you'd expect: of the year's rain on the coast and only in Rayalaseema.

The rest comes from the north-east monsoon and the cyclones of October–December. October is actually the coast's wettest month (184 mm), and Rayalaseema gets of its rain in these three months. So June–September alone doesn't tell you if it was a good year.

Every bar is one year. The swings are big: a typical year is 18–20% above or below the average.

Is it getting wetter or drier? The 10-year average wanders around but the straight trend line is almost flat: about mm per decade, with p = . That's not a real trend, just noise.

What matters more is how often a year goes badly. Using the IMD rule (more than 20% below normal), Rayalaseema had 18 deficient years in 117. The coast had 12, with 2002 the driest (703 mm).

These are the 13 districts in the crop data. Nine are coastal and four are in Rayalaseema.

Rice is AP's biggest crop by area (about 24.5 lakh hectares a year). The two Godavari districts, Krishna and Guntur grow 63.5% of it, almost all on canal water from the deltas.

Groundnut is second (about 13.8 lakh ha) and it is a Rayalaseema crop. Anantapur alone produced 39% of the state's groundnut, mostly rain-fed.

And since I'm from Guntur, I had to check: Guntur grew 57.7% of all of AP's dry chillies between 1997 and 2014.

Now the main question. Each dot is one year of kharif groundnut in the four Rayalaseema districts. The more it rained from June to September, the more groundnut each hectare gave: r = 0.77 (p < 0.001).

Look at 2006, 2007 and 2008. With about 340 mm of monsoon rain in 2006 and 2008, farmers got about 0.23 t/ha. In 2007, with 665 mm, they got 1.37 t/ha. That's a six-fold swing in one year.

Coastal rice is a different story. Its yield has basically no link with that year's rainfall (r = −0.27, not significant). The delta is canal irrigated, so the water comes from the rivers and dams, not straight from the sky.

Irrigation is what separates the two regions. A rain-fed Rayalaseema farmer carries the full risk of a bad monsoon; a delta farmer mostly doesn't.

How I did it

Everything is in a Jupyter notebook with pandas, SciPy and seaborn. The charts on this page are drawn with D3 from a small JSON file that scripts/build_story_data.py makes from the same data, so the numbers match the notebook.

  • Cleaning. The crop file had 61 "Whole Year" rows that were just Kharif + Rabi added together (mostly 2013–14), which made 2014 rice look double. I removed them, plus 67 rows with no production value.
  • Deficient / excess years follow the IMD rule: more than 20% below or above the long-term mean of that sub-division.
  • Trend is a simple linear regression of annual rainfall on year. Correlations are Pearson's r between yearly yield (production ÷ area) and rainfall.

Limits

  • The crop data covers only 18 years (1997–2014), so each correlation is based on 18 points.
  • Rainfall is at sub-division level, not district level.
  • Correlation is not causation. Prices, pests, seed and the area sown also move yield.
